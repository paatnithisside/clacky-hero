# Part I — The strategic story

## 1. The problem Clacky should own

Makers rarely lose a whole day to one enormous task. They lose it in fragments: opening another utility, checking whether an AI agent finished, trimming a recording, preparing captions, switching to analytics, and trying to understand whether their work is getting results.

Each interruption seems harmless. Together, they make a maker's working day feel scattered.

Clacky should not be positioned as “a collection of 25+ features.” A collection is a product inventory, not a reason to buy. The stronger story is that useful parts of the maker's workflow can sit closer together, with less friction between creating something, showing it, and learning from the response.

### The transformation

**Before:** The maker juggles separate tools, repeats small steps, checks progress too often, and struggles to connect activity with outcomes.

**After:** The maker has a simpler Mac setup, can move from recording to sharing with fewer steps, gets notified about supported agent activity, and has a clearer view of traffic, funnels, and revenue.

This is a promise of reduced friction and better visibility—not a guarantee that Clacky automatically makes someone more productive, increases conversion, or grows revenue.

## 2. The message hierarchy

1. **Emotional benefit:** Spend more attention on the work that matters.
2. **Practical outcome:** Create, share, monitor, and understand work from a more connected Mac workflow.
3. **Product proof:** Real Clacky interfaces: Studio, agent notifications, analytics, utilities, and scheduling.
4. **Personality:** Warm, playful, tactile details, including Clacky's sound and world-building elements.
5. **Conversion:** Start the free trial with a clear explanation of what is included and when billing begins.

## 3. Who the homepage is for

The homepage has to speak to a broad maker audience without turning into a vague promise for everyone.

- **Indie makers and solo founders:** Want to ship and promote work without maintaining an unwieldy stack of tools.
- **Developers using AI coding agents:** Want fewer unnecessary status checks and useful ways to interact with their Mac workflow.
- **Creators and product teams:** Want to record, polish, caption, and share product demos with less manual post-production.
- **Small-product operators:** Want to understand where visitors arrive from, where they drop off, and which sources contribute to sales.

Use the creation-to-learning workflow as the unifying story. Use section-level messaging to make each distinct job clear.

# Part III — Visual design direction

## 4. The visual idea

### Concept name: The Maker's Cockpit

**Main idea:** Show a maker's work moving from creation to sharing to learning, through one carefully composed environment of real Clacky interfaces.

The hero should not be a generic laptop mockup, a random happy founder, a pile of floating icons, or a giant illustration with no product evidence. Those devices are familiar enough to be invisible. The visitor should understand the product's value in a few seconds and feel curious enough to explore.

The hierarchy is:

1. **Primary visual:** Clacky's Studio showing a real screen recording becoming a polished, captioned demo.
2. **Secondary visual:** A small AI-agent completion notification, placed as a quiet proof point.
3. **Tertiary visual:** A compact analytics panel showing traffic-source, funnel, or revenue insight.
4. **Personality accent:** One tiny Clackyland/Blip detail, or a cursor ripple, used sparingly.

The main interface is the hero. The illustration is supporting cast. Never reverse those priorities.

## 5. The emotional target

A visitor should feel these emotions in order:

1. **Welcome:** The site feels considered, warm, and easy to enter.
2. **Recognition:** “This is the annoying stuff I deal with every day.”
3. **Relief:** “Those jobs could sit closer together.”
4. **Curiosity:** “I want to see how this actually works.”
5. **Trust:** “The product is real, the claims are specific, and the pricing is clear.”
6. **Confidence:** “Trying it is low-friction and I understand the next step.”

Do not use visual chaos to dramatize the problem. Do not use an exaggerated before/after that makes the customer look incompetent. The friction should feel familiar and human; the proposed solution should feel calm, capable, and slightly delightful.

## 6. Hero layout: exact creative direction

### Desktop composition

Design for a wide desktop canvas first, but keep the first viewport legible at common laptop heights.

- **Overall split:** Approximately 40% copy / 60% visual on a spacious desktop. Adjust naturally rather than treating this as a rigid grid.
- **Copy side:** Eyebrow, headline, two-to-three-line description, primary CTA, secondary CTA, then a short reassurance/trust line.
- **Visual side:** Layered product windows with clear depth. The Studio window is the largest object. Analytics and notification cards are supporting details.
- **Breathing room:** Use generous space around the headline and focal interface. Do not fill every corner because empty space makes someone nervous.
- **Baseline:** Align the visual's strongest edge with the text block, and balance the total composition so it doesn't look like a screenshot pile sliding off the page.

### The primary Studio window

Build a large, credible interface card that feels like a real Mac app window rather than a generic SaaS dashboard.

Inside it, show:

- A believable product walkthrough preview with a clean frame.
- Captions visibly aligned to the video content.
- A compact timeline or transcript-editing cue.
- A cursor ripple or click highlight at the relevant interaction.
- A clear export/completion state, where appropriate.

The viewer should visually understand “I can turn a screen recording into a presentable demo” without having to read a paragraph.

**Do not** shrink the interface until its text is unreadable. Use fewer, larger interface cues rather than cramming a complete product screenshot into the hero.

### Supporting analytics panel

Position this panel slightly behind or below Studio so it reads as a second layer in the same workspace.

Show only three or four useful data points, such as:

- Visitors or pageviews.
- One traffic source.
- A small funnel step or conversion goal.
- A revenue metric, where the connected integration supports it.

Use real product UI if available. If sample values are needed, label them as sample/demo data and do not present them as Clacky customer outcomes.

### AI-agent notification

Place a compact notification near the edge of the composition, like a real macOS notification. Keep it small enough that Studio remains the focal point.

Example notification copy for the *design mockup only*:

**Task completed**  
Your coding agent has finished.

Do not imply that Clacky itself made the task complete faster. It helps the user notice supported events; the agent performs the underlying coding work.

### Personality detail

Introduce one small piece of delight: a cursor ripple travelling across a panel, a tiny Blip character at the edge of the workspace, or a distant harbour-town detail tucked into the environment.

Choose one. Do not use every whimsical idea at once.

## 7. Hero interaction and motion script

Motion should demonstrate the workflow and reward attention. It should not turn the first viewport into a carnival ride.

### Recommended sequence

**0.0–0.4 seconds: Arrive**

- Headline and CTA appear immediately or with a very short opacity/position transition.
- Do not hide the headline behind a full-screen loader.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences from the start.

**0.4–1.2 seconds: Establish the product**

- Studio window settles into its final position using a subtle spring or ease-out.
- Supporting windows move a few pixels into place to create depth.
- Shadows resolve softly; avoid dramatic spinning, 3D flips, or elastic overshoot.

**1.2–2.4 seconds: Show the transformation**

- The video preview advances briefly.
- A caption line appears or the timeline selection moves.
- A cursor ripple signals a click or highlighted feature.
- The demo reaches a polished/ready-to-share state.

**2.4–3.2 seconds: Add proof of the wider workflow**

- The agent notification appears once and settles.
- The analytics card reveals a simple source/conversion cue.
- A small label or visual connector can imply “Make → Share → Learn.”

**After 3.2 seconds: Rest**

- Stop the animation in a beautiful, composed final state.
- Do not loop incessantly. If a loop is used, it should be slow, subtle, and non-distracting.
- Let the visitor control or replay the demonstration if possible.

### Motion rules

- Use short, purposeful transitions, generally around 160–420 ms for interface state changes.
- Use a restrained spring for floating windows, not for text or critical UI controls.
- Keep cursor animations local to the element they explain.
- Avoid continuous bobbing of all elements, endless marquee text, constant sparkle particles, and multiple simultaneous loops.
- Disable non-essential animation when `prefers-reduced-motion` is enabled.
- Motion must not block clicking the primary CTA or delay content rendering.

## 8. Alternate visual concept: The cozy maker's world

Clackyland and the sound features add personality. Use them to make the page memorable, not as the primary explanation of the core product.

A soft, miniature harbour world can live behind the interface at low contrast, or appear later in the “Your Mac can feel a little more alive” section. Think of it as a small discovery the visitor finds after understanding the tool.

### Art direction

- Miniature harbour town, tiny lighthouse, stacked houses, little boats, gentle water shapes.
- Soft, tactile surfaces rather than shiny plastic 3D.
- Warm ivory, muted sky blue, sage, peach, and soft lilac.
- Small-scale details that reward a second look without creating visual noise.
- A tiny Blip, if using the character, should retain the approved official design. Do not invent a mascot variant and pass it off as official.

### Where to use it

- A quiet background world behind the product windows.
- A small visual bridge into the Clackyland section.
- A footer illustration or section-transition moment.
- A gentle hover or discovery interaction in the personality section.

### Where not to use it

- As a replacement for real product UI in the hero.
- As a huge full-screen animated environment that pushes the CTA below the fold.
- As a metaphor so abstract that visitors have to decode what the software does.

## 9. Colour, type, material, and lighting

Treat the following as a starting art direction, not confirmed official brand tokens. Reconcile it with the current Clacky app and website before implementation.

### Suggested palette

| Role | Direction | Example hex |
|---|---|---|
| Main canvas | Warm ivory / soft paper | `#F4F1E8` |
| Main text | Charcoal with a little warmth | `#242720` |
| Secondary text | Muted grey-green | `#62645D` |
| Primary accent | Soft sky blue | `#A8C3E1` |

| Playful accent | Soft lilac | `#BDB4DE` |

**Colour rule:** Use neutrals for the structural elements and reserve colour for states, highlights, and personality. Do not give every floating panel a different bright colour. Make the page feel like a calm workspace with small moments of joy.

### Typography

- Pair a strong, modern sans serif with an easy-reading body face. If the product already has a brand typeface, start there.
- The hero headline should feel confident and human, not like enterprise procurement software.
- Keep the hero body copy to a comfortable measure; avoid long full-width lines.
- Use a restrained type scale with obvious hierarchy: hero headline, section heading, card heading, body, caption.
- Use sentence case for most headings. Avoid unnecessary ALL CAPS, apart from short section labels.
- Do not place small text over textured backgrounds or behind translucent panels.

### Surface language

- Native Mac-window cues where they add credibility: compact window chrome, clear hierarchy, familiar controls.
- Rounded corners, but not every surface needs to be a giant pill.
- Soft shadows with realistic falloff. Avoid heavy drop shadows that make the interface look like a stack of cardboard.
- Thin, low-contrast separators for utility panels.
- Slight texture may appear in an illustration, but keep product UI crisp and legible.
- Use glass only sparingly. Frosted glass should never reduce the readability of actual UI.

### Depth and lighting

- Use one primary light direction across the composition.
- Create depth primarily through scale, overlap, and low-elevation shadows—not strong perspective distortion.
- If the product windows float, keep the baseline coherent and avoid random angles.
- Avoid a dramatic neon rim light, chrome materials, or glossy 3D hardware that contradicts the cozy native-Mac feeling.

- Show the harbour town as an art-directed illustration or short, purposeful scene.
- Let one or two elements respond to a hover or click, not the entire world at once.
- If any sound or movement is decorative, it must be easy to ignore.

**Purpose:** Give the visitor a memorable emotional reason to like the product after the practical value is clear.

### Testimonials section

**Visual:** Calm, editorial quote cards. Prioritise actual user words over giant avatar illustrations or star-rating decorations.

- Use real names/handles only when permission and attribution are verified.
- Avoid fake headshots, fake company logos, and invented metrics.
- Keep the cards readable and visually consistent.

**Purpose:** Reduce uncertainty using authentic evidence.

### Pricing section

**Visual:** Clear, highly readable cards with the plan differences visible without a treasure hunt.

- Highlight the plan that aligns with the product's business goals, but do not use manipulative countdowns or false scarcity.
- Make billing cadence, pageview limits, website/social account limits, trial terms, and cancellation language clear.
- Put the main CTA in the same position on each card.
- Show the lifetime plan only with its distinct one-time-payment terms made unmistakable.

**Purpose:** Make the purchase decision feel straightforward rather than engineered to confuse.

### Privacy section

**Visual:** A simple local-vs-online diagram or a small privacy checklist using actual product behavior.

- Separate local workflows from online integrations.
- Use plain-language labels, not a shield icon alone as a substitute for explanation.
- Ensure every privacy statement matches the current technical implementation.

**Purpose:** Build trust by being specific.

### Maker story

**Visual:** A real photo, work-in-progress screenshot, changelog fragment, or development artifact from the maker, if available and approved.

- Prefer authentic maker evidence to a stock “founder at laptop” photo.
- Keep it intimate, tidy, and grounded.
- Link to the public build-in-public channel only if it is active and maintained.

**Purpose:** Make the independent-product story tangible.

### Final CTA

**Visual:** Return to the calm core interface, not a brand-new illustration. Let a small version of the Studio preview and one restrained personality detail accompany the final promise.

**Purpose:** Reaffirm the value and give the visitor a clean next step.

## 14. Responsive behavior

### Large desktop

- Two-column hero with the visual occupying roughly 60% of the available content width.
- Allow layered windows to extend beyond their immediate container only when there is enough breathing room.
- Keep the primary Studio panel largest and fully readable.

### Laptop / small desktop

- Reduce the number of visible UI layers before shrinking all the UI.
- Maintain readable headline wrapping and a clear primary CTA.
- Keep the hero height sensible; the first section should invite scrolling, not consume several full viewport heights.

### Tablet

- Use a slightly more balanced split or stack the visual underneath the copy.
- Keep analytics and notification cards connected to Studio, but reduce overlap and shadow strength.
- Keep touch targets generous.

### Mobile

- Stack in this order: eyebrow → headline → body → primary CTA → secondary CTA → reassurance → product visual.
- Show one main Studio screenshot with one notification or analytics detail, not a miniature desktop collage.
- Use a crop designed for portrait screens rather than scaling the desktop image down.
- Ensure the headline does not wrap into a tall, awkward stack.
- Keep the CTA visible without requiring the visitor to scroll through a full-screen animation.
- Remove decorative parallax and high-motion interactions that don't work well on touch.

## 15. Accessibility and performance

A delightful site that causes motion sickness, hides its CTA, or takes too long to load is not delightful. It's an expensive screensaver.

- Follow `prefers-reduced-motion` for all non-essential movement.
- Never autoplay sound. All audio needs an obvious user-initiated control.
- Ensure keyboard access to all navigation, toggles, demo playback controls, and sound previews.
- Provide meaningful alternative text for informative images; mark purely decorative art as decorative.
- Check text, buttons, and UI callouts for sufficient contrast.
- Make focus states visible and consistent.
- Lazy-load lower-page artwork and video; prioritise the hero's critical UI.
- Use a static poster for the hero video and avoid making essential copy depend on video playback.
- Avoid heavy WebGL or 3D in the hero unless it meaningfully improves comprehension and performs well on lower-powered Macs.
- Do not let motion, animation, or large images delay Largest Contentful Paint or prevent the visitor from clicking the CTA.
- Respect data-saving and low-power settings where practical.

# Part V — Copy and UX rules

## 16. Message and content rules

- Lead with outcomes, then explain the features that deliver them.
- Keep the hero focused on one idea: makers can create, share, and understand their work with less workflow fragmentation.
- Use section-level messages for individual audiences. Do not force every feature into the hero headline.
- Describe Clacky as a companion or connected workspace, not as a magical AI platform that autonomously does all the work.
- Say “helps reduce,” “makes it easier to,” or “gives you visibility into” when the result depends on user behavior.
- Avoid quantified productivity, conversion, or revenue promises until there is reliable, documented evidence.
- Make the difference between local features and online features understandable.
- Keep the primary CTA consistent unless testing shows a better alternative.
- Repeat reassuring trial language near major purchase moments, but do not place multiple competing buttons everywhere.

## 17. CTA system

**Primary CTA:** Try Clacky free for 7 days

**Secondary CTA:** Explore the features

Use the primary label consistently in the hero, navigation, pricing section, and final CTA, with a sensible shortened form in tight spaces if needed.

The CTA destination must clearly explain:
- Whether account creation is required.
- What is included in the trial.
- Whether a payment method is required up front.
- When billing starts.
- How and when to cancel.

Only state “no payment is charged during the trial” if the current billing flow guarantees it.

## 18. Claims and trust checklist

Before launch, verify every one of these claims against the current product, pricing system, and privacy documentation:

- [ ] Current version number in announcement bar.
- [ ] “Every feature included” language across all plans.
- [ ] Number of Macs covered by each licence.
- [ ] macOS minimum versions for the main app, screenshot tools, and recording/captions.
- [ ] Trial length, payment-method requirement, billing start, cancellation process, and refund terms.
- [ ] Monthly, annual, and lifetime prices and discount calculations.
- [ ] Pageview allowance and limits for websites, social accounts, scheduled posts, and AI questions.
- [ ] Current supported AI integrations and exact MCP permissions.
- [ ] What data stays local versus what is sent to online services.
- [ ] Cookie-free analytics and the precise meaning of “no tracking cookies.”
- [ ] Revenue integrations, read-only status, and sales attribution limits.
- [ ] Tool-stack savings calculation and competitor comparison methodology.
- [ ] Testimonials, names, quote accuracy, and publication permissions.
- [ ] Apple signing/notarization language.
- [ ] Copyright year and maker biography.

If a claim can't be verified, remove it or rewrite it more narrowly. Trust is too valuable to spend on a sentence that sounded good in a draft.

# Part VI — Success criteria

## 19. What the hero is meant to achieve

The hero should help a new visitor answer these questions quickly:

1. What is Clacky?
2. Is it for someone like me?
3. What changes in my working day if I use it?
4. What is the product actually like?
5. Why should I trust the claims?
6. What happens if I click the CTA?

If the visual is beautiful but fails those questions, it's an art project, not an effective hero.

## 20. Metrics to instrument and watch

Measure conversion honestly. Don't attribute every improvement to the new design without a test or baseline.

### Primary funnel metrics

- Hero primary CTA click-through rate.
- Trial-start rate from homepage sessions.
- Homepage-to-trial conversion rate.
- Trial-to-paid conversion rate.
- Conversion by device and traffic source.

### Experience quality metrics

- Time until the hero headline and primary CTA are usable.
- Scroll depth into Studio, Analytics, and Pricing sections.
- Feature-section engagement, measured in privacy-conscious ways.
- Hero video interaction rate, if a video is used.
- Mobile CTA visibility and interaction rate.
- Performance metrics, especially Largest Contentful Paint, Cumulative Layout Shift, and Interaction to Next Paint.

### User outcome metrics to learn over time

- Time from recording a demo to publishing it.
- Demos or product updates shared per week.
- Manual AI-agent status checks per task.
- Percentage of users who configure an analytics goal.
- Percentage of users who connect revenue data, where appropriate.
- Trial users who reach an important activation event.

Use qualitative interviews or short user surveys to learn whether visitors understand the story. Quantitative conversion alone can reveal what happened, but not necessarily why.

# Final creative direction

**Build a landing page that feels like opening a beautifully organised, slightly playful Mac workspace.** It should communicate calm competence before whimsy, and use whimsy to make the experience memorable after the value is clear.

The hero's largest object is a real Studio interface transforming a recording into something shareable. A small agent-completion notification and a restrained analytics panel show the wider workflow. One tiny cursor ripple or Clackyland detail creates delight. Warm surfaces, readable type, soft depth, and purposeful motion make the page feel carefully crafted.

The visitor should leave the first viewport thinking:

**“This looks considered. I understand what it does. It could make my working day less fragmented. I want to see it in action.”**

That is the standard for both the copy and the visual design.
